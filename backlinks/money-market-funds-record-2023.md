After SVB failed in March, I moved our emergency fund out of a big-bank savings account paying 0.01%. It went into a government money market fund yielding a little over 5%. ## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Is it smart to keep an emergency fund in a money market fund instead of a savings account?

For most people with a brokerage account, yes, as long as you understand the trade-offs.

The case for it is the yield gap. In 2023, government money market funds paid a little over 5%, the highest since 2007, while many big-bank savings accounts still paid 0.01%. On a $40,000 emergency fund, that is roughly $2,000 a year in interest versus about $4. That gap is a big reason money market fund assets climbed to a record of nearly $6 trillion that year.

What you give up:

1. FDIC insurance. Money market funds are not bank deposits. Government and Treasury funds hold T-bills, agency debt and repurchase agreements backed by Treasuries, so the risk of losing principal is very low. It is not guaranteed, though.

2. Instant access. Selling shares and moving the cash to checking usually takes one business day. Keep a small buffer in checking for true same-day emergencies.

3. Rate certainty. The yield floats with the Fed. When rates fall, your income falls too, and faster than it would with a CD.

How to choose:
- Stick to government or Treasury funds for emergency money. Prime funds add a little yield and a little credit risk you do not need here.
- Check the expense ratio. Anything above about 0.20% eats a meaningful share of the yield.
- If you live in a high-tax state, a Treasury-heavy fund may be partly exempt from state income tax. After tax, that can beat a high-yield savings account.
- Use the fund at the brokerage you already have so transfers stay simple.

A high-yield savings account is still a fine choice if you want FDIC coverage and same-day transfers. The real mistake is leaving the money at 0.01%.
