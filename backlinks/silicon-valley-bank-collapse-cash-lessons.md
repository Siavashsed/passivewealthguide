A friend who runs a small business told me that Friday morning that his payroll account was suddenly in limbo. He had assumed cash was the boring, risk-free part of his finances. It turned out that FDIC coverage caps at $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category, and a lot of people had balances well above that or spread across accounts they thought were separate.

- I check which institution actually holds my money, not just the app I log into
- I know my coverage limit at each bank
- I keep a separate cushion at a second institution so I can reach cash if one goes down

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What should I learn about keeping cash safe from the Silicon Valley Bank collapse?

The biggest lesson from the Silicon Valley Bank collapse is that "safe" is a specific, limited promise, not a general feeling. Most people, myself included, had never read what that promise actually covered.

Here is what I would take away from it.

1. Know your FDIC limits. Coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Single accounts, joint accounts, and certain retirement accounts are treated as different categories, so structure matters. Balances above the limit at one bank are exposed.

2. Know who actually holds your money. Many apps and fintech tools sit on top of partner banks. Your protection depends on the bank behind the interface, and on whether the app's setup passes coverage through to you.

3. Keep liquidity in more than one place. Even when deposits are eventually made whole, access can be frozen for days. A small emergency cushion at a second institution means one bank's problem does not become your payroll or rent problem.

4. Separate operating cash from savings. Money you need next week should be easy to reach and clearly insured. Money you will not touch for a year can be placed more deliberately, for example in Treasury bills or a brokerage money market fund, with a clear understanding of how each is protected.

5. Do not chase yield blindly. An unusually high rate is a reason to ask how the institution makes its money, not a reason to relax.

None of this requires panic. It requires a short audit: list every account, the institution behind it, the balance, and the coverage that applies.
