Annual stuff like car insurance, gifts, vet bills and the occasional repair never showed up in my monthly mental math.

The method I use now is simple:

1. Total real spending from the past 12+ months of statements, not from a budget.
2. Subtract any income that keeps arriving without a job (pension, rental net, a small royalty, whatever).
3. Divide the gap by a withdrawal rate you'd actually be comfortable with. For me that's closer to 3.5% than 4%.

That usually lands between 25x and 30x annual spending. The gap between those two multiples is a lot of money, so the withdrawal rate deserves more thought than it usually gets.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do I calculate how much passive income I need to become financially independent?

The short version: take your real annual spending, subtract any income that will keep arriving without a job, and divide what's left by a withdrawal rate you're comfortable with. For most people that comes out to 25 to 30 times what they spend in a year.

The word that matters there is real. Most people start with their budget, and budgets leave things out. When I first did this I went through 14 months of bank and credit card statements, and my actual spending was noticeably higher than I'd assumed. Annual and irregular costs were the culprits: insurance renewals, car repairs, holidays, gifts, medical bills, replacing a laptop. None of these feel like monthly spending, but they are real spending.

Here's the process I recommend:

1. Export at least 12 months of statements from every account and card. Add everything up, including the irregular items.
2. Subtract reliable non-job income. That could be a pension, net rental income, Social Security later on, or any steady side income you expect to continue without active work.
3. Choose a withdrawal rate. The classic 4% rule means 25x your spending gap. If you want a bigger margin for a longer retirement or bad early market years, 3.5% means about 28.5x, and 3.33% means 30x.
4. Multiply. A $50,000 gap needs about $1.25M at 4% and about $1.43M at 3.5%.

That difference is why the withdrawal rate deserves real thought. Pick the one that lets you sleep, not the one that makes the number look smallest.

I wrote a longer walkthrough of this method, including how I handled irregular expenses, here: https://siavashsed.github.io/passivewealthguide/passive-income-number-financial-independence/

One last tip: run the numbers again every year. Spending changes over time, and your target should change with it.
