**Title:** I ran the numbers on 30 years of $500/month at 7%, and the last decade earns more than the first two combined

I keep seeing people in their 20s and early 30s get discouraged because their balance barely moves after a few years of steady contributions. I felt the same, so I worked out a simple scenario to see where the growth actually happens.

Assume $500 a month, a 7% annual return compounded monthly, and no lump sums:

- After 10 years: about $86,500 (you put in $60,000)
- After 20 years: about $260,500 (you put in $120,000)
- After 30 years: about $610,000 (you put in $180,000)

The jump from year 20 to year 30 is roughly $350,000. The first twenty years together come to about $260,000. Each decade has the same $60,000 in contributions. The only difference is the size of the base doing the compounding.

For me the practical takeaway was that the early years aren't where you see results. They're where you build the base that produces the results later. Stopping contributions or cashing out in year 8 because it "isn't working" cuts off the part of the curve that matters most.

I'm curious whether anyone here has actually lived through that late-stage acceleration, and how it compared to what you expected.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why does compound interest seem so slow in the first years of investing?

It seems slow because in the early years it really is slow, at least in dollar terms. Compound interest earns returns on your existing balance. When the balance is small, even a good return is a small number. Your own contributions do almost all of the visible work at first.

Here is a concrete example. Say you invest $500 a month at a 7% annual return:

- Year 10: about $86,500, of which $60,000 is your own money
- Year 20: about $260,500, of which $120,000 is your own money
- Year 30: about $610,000, of which $180,000 is your own money

In the first decade growth adds only about $26,500 on top of what you put in. Between year 20 and year 30, the balance grows by roughly $350,000. That is more than the first twenty years produced combined, even though you contributed the same $60,000 in each decade. The only thing that changed is the size of the base.

I learned this the hard way. A few years into contributing to a Roth IRA, I looked at my balance and was frustrated by how little it had moved. I was judging a 30-year process by its first few years, and that is the stretch where it looks worst.

A few practical points follow from the math:

1. Starting early matters more than starting big, because it gives you more years in the steep part of the curve.
2. Interrupting contributions or withdrawing early hurts more than it looks, because you shrink the base that drives the later growth.
3. Fees and lower returns compound too, so small differences grow large over decades.
