Starting in March 2024 I split my emergency fund between a 5-year credit union CD and a few other cash options. Watching how each one actually behaved changed the order I'd recommend now.

Here's where I landed for 2027:

1. Treasury money market fund at the brokerage you already use. You can get the money out the same day or the next, there's no early withdrawal penalty, and the yield follows short-term rates without you doing anything.

2. If you pay real state income tax, add a T-bill ladder on top. Treasury interest is exempt from state and local tax and CD interest isn't. In a high-tax state, that difference can wipe out a CD's headline rate advantage.

3. Buy a CD only when you want to lock in a rate you think is about to disappear. That's the one job they still do well.

What bugged me about the long CD was the trade-off. I was giving up flexibility on money that exists for emergencies, and the extra yield rarely made up for that.

Does anyone here still prefer CDs for an emergency fund? Is there a case where they clearly win that I'm overlooking?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Where should I keep my emergency fund in 2027: CDs, Treasury bills, or a money market fund?

For most people, the best answer is a Treasury money market fund at the brokerage you already use. Then add T-bills if state taxes matter to you, and use CDs only for one specific purpose. Here's why I'd go in that order.

Treasury money market fund first. Your emergency fund has one job: to be there when something goes wrong. A Treasury money market fund lets you get your money the same day or the next, has no early withdrawal penalty, and its yield follows short-term rates on its own. If it's at your existing brokerage, there's no new account to open and nothing to manage.

T-bills second, if you pay real state income tax. Interest on Treasury bills is exempt from state and local income tax, and CD interest is fully taxable at the state level. In a high-tax state, a T-bill with a slightly lower headline rate can still pay you more after tax. A simple ladder, with bills maturing every few weeks or months, keeps part of the money coming due regularly so you're never stuck.

CDs last, and only for one reason. A CD makes sense when you want to lock in a rate you think is about to disappear. The trade-off is flexibility. If you need the money early, the penalty can eat the extra yield you were chasing, and that's a bad fit for money whose whole purpose is unpredictable timing.

I learned this order slowly. Starting in March 2024, I split my own emergency money between a 5-year credit union CD and other options, and the long CD was the part I regretted. I wrote up the full comparison, with the after-tax math, here: https://siavashsed.github.io/passivewealthguide/treasury-bills-cds-money-market-2027/

In short, keep it liquid, account for state tax, and only lock money up when you have a clear reason to.
