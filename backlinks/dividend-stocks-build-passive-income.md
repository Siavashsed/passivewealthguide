## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do dividend stocks actually build passive income over time?

Dividend stocks build passive income in two ways at once: they pay you a share of company profits, usually every quarter, and the good ones raise that payment year after year. That means the same number of shares sends you more cash than it did before, without you doing anything.

I'm Ryan Mitchell, and I'll use my own start as an example. In March 2019 I bought 40 shares of Johnson & Johnson in a Fidelity account. The first dividend was a little under $40. Honestly, it felt tiny. But that small deposit showed me the mechanism, and the mechanism is what matters.

Here's how it compounds:

1. You buy shares of a company that pays a dividend.
2. The company pays you cash each quarter.
3. You reinvest that cash (most brokerages let you automate this), which buys more shares.
4. Those new shares pay their own dividends next quarter.
5. The company raises its payout over time, so every share earns more.

Early on, your own contributions drive most of the growth, so progress feels slow. After a few years, the dividends themselves start adding meaningful new shares, and the curve bends upward. That's the "slowly, then all at once" feeling.

A few practical points:

- Don't chase the highest yield. An unusually high yield can signal a company in trouble and a dividend that may get cut.
- Look at the payout history. Companies that have raised dividends for many consecutive years have usually shown durable cash flow.
- Diversify across sectors so one bad industry doesn't gut your income.
- Keep contributing regularly, even small amounts. Consistency beats timing.
- Mind taxes. Dividends in a taxable account are taxed, so consider where you hold them.

Dividends are never guaranteed, and no stock is risk free. But as a long-term habit, it's one of the more understandable ways to build income.
