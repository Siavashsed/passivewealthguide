## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Are high dividend yield stocks a good hedge against inflation?

Usually not, at least not on their own. A high yield protects your income only if the payout grows, and many of the highest yielders grow slowly or not at all.

Here is the math that changed my mind. Put $10,000 into a stock yielding 6% with a flat dividend and you collect $600 a year. At 3% inflation, that $600 buys roughly $446 worth of goods after ten years. Your statement says your income is steady. Your grocery bill says otherwise.

Now take a stock yielding 2.5% that raises its dividend about 10% a year. Year one you get $250, which looks worse. After ten raises you are collecting about $648 a year on the same original $10,000, and after fifteen raises around $1,044. That is before reinvesting anything.

The catch is that the growth has to be real. When I evaluate a dividend grower I look at three things:

1. Dividend growth rate over 5 and 10 years, not just the latest raise.
2. Payout ratio, with room to keep raising without straining earnings.
3. Free cash flow coverage, because dividends are paid in cash, not adjusted earnings.

High yield is not automatically bad. If you are retired and need income now, a higher starting yield can make sense, and some REITs and utilities do grow their payouts. But if your horizon is ten years or more, the size of the raise usually matters more than the starting number.
