Your 6% Yield Is Losing to Inflation. Chase the Raise Instead

How to build a dividend growth portfolio that beats inflation: why growth rate beats yield, the screen I use, and the trade-offs nobody mentions.

Siavash Sadighi November 16, 2026 4 min read

To build a dividend growth portfolio that beats inflation, stop shopping for the fattest yield. Buy companies whose payouts grow faster than prices do, then reinvest every cent for at least a decade. In June 2022 I was standing in my kitchen at 8:30 a.m., refreshing the Bureau of Labor Statistics CPI page, when inflation came in at 9.1%. My first thought went to my "safe" 7% yield REIT, which hadn't raised its payout since 2019. I did the math on the back of a grocery receipt and realized I was losing about 2% of my real income every year while feeling clever about it.

Here's the math that changed how I invest. If you put $10,000 into a stock yielding 6% that never raises its payout, you collect $600 a year forever. Now take the same money and buy a 3% yielder that grows its dividend 8% a year. You're at about $600 by year 10 and roughly $880 by year 15. Meanwhile, at 3% inflation, that flat $600 will buy what about $385 buys today. The gap gets lopsided... fast. My first quarterly payout from this approach was a boring $47 check that quietly rewired my retirement, and the slow climb did the work, not the starting number.

Dividend Growth Rate Beats Starting Yield Almost Every Time

This is my position: the growth rate of the dividend matters more than the yield you start with, at least if you have ten or more years before you need the income. A company paying 2.5% that raises its payout 9% a year will out-earn a flat 6% payer within about a decade. It's usually the healthier business, too.

The long-run data agrees. Hartford Funds and Ned Davis Research looked at 1973 through 2024 and found that S&P 500 companies that grew or started dividends returned around 10% a year, with less volatility than the index. Non-payers managed closer to 4%. Companies that cut their dividends did worst of all.

The quick screen I actually use

Years ago I picked up the Chowder Rule from the Seeking Alpha crowd. You add a stock's current yield to its five-year dividend growth rate. If the total reaches 12 or more (8 for utilities), the stock stays on my list. It's crude. But it weeds out yield traps quickly, and it makes inflation the hurdle you measure against, because a score of 12 leaves plenty of room above a 3% CPI.

What I Check Before a Stock Earns a Spot

When I rebuilt my own portfolio in early 2023, I cut it from 31 holdings to 19. Every stock that stayed had to pass four checks:

  • At least 10 straight years of dividend raises. The S&P 500 Dividend Aristocrats require 25, which makes it a good place to look, though not the only one.
  • A payout ratio under about 60% of earnings, so the company can keep raising through a bad year.
  • Free cash flow that covers the dividend, because earnings can be dressed up and cash usually can't.
  • Debt the business could pay off in under four years of operating cash flow.

Confession time. Back in 2018 I bought a mall REIT yielding 11.4% because a guy on a podcast called it "mispriced," and I told my brother over dinner it was practically a bond with upside. Fourteen months later it cut the dividend in half. He still texts me "11.4%" whenever I offer him financial advice, which is fair. And honestly, I'm not sure the four checks above would have saved me... the payout ratio looked fine on paper, and I really wanted to believe it.

So the screen isn't magic. It tilts the odds in your favor. These days I treat any yield above 7% as a question that needs an answer before I'll call it an opportunity.

ETFs, Individual Stocks, or a Mix of Both

You don't need to pick 20 stocks to do this well. Schwab's SCHD and Vanguard's VIG both give you a pre-screened dividend growth basket for about $5 to $6 a year per $10,000 invested. They aren't twins, though. VIG leans toward faster growers with a lower yield, usually under 2%. SCHD has paid closer to 3.5% with slower raises.

I keep roughly 60% in funds and 40% in individual names. The funds are ballast. The individual stocks let me overweight businesses I actually understand, like Costco or a dull freight railroad, and when one of them stumbles I can harvest the tax loss.

Here's the trade-off. Individual stocks cost you about an hour a quarter reading earnings reports, and you'll be tempted to tinker. If that sounds miserable, go 100% funds and you'll give up very little. I wrote more about the patience side of this in why I trusted dividend stocks with my retirement.

Where This Strategy Lets You Down

Dividend growth investing has real weak spots. If you pretend it doesn't, you'll bail at the worst possible moment. It lags in bull markets led by tech, because the biggest winners pay little or nothing. In 2023 my dividend sleeve trailed the S&P 500 by something like 15 points, and I spent most of that December wondering whether I was the stubborn guy at the party.

A common misconception is that dividends are a bonus stacked on top of your returns. They aren't. On the ex-dividend date the share price drops by roughly the amount of the payout, so the dividend is your own money moving from one pocket to another. The real engine is the business growing underneath. In a taxable account you also pay tax on those payments every year, even when you reinvest them. Qualified dividends get the lower capital gains rates, but the tax still drags on your returns in a way a non-payer's doesn't.

That's why most of my dividend growers sit in a Roth IRA, and why I keep a cash buffer somewhere else so that a 30% market drop never forces me to sell. If your emergency fund is shaky, fix that first. The four savings numbers that matter more than your APY is a good place to start.

Before you close this tab, open your brokerage account and make a two-column list with each holding's current yield and its five-year dividend growth rate. Add the two numbers together. Anything under 12 (or 8 for a utility) goes on a watch list, and anything yielding over 7% with a flat payout gets a hard look this week. Then check that dividend reinvestment is turned on for every position you plan to keep. For beating inflation, that one setting has done more for me than any stock I've ever picked.

Frequently asked questions

What dividend growth rate do I need to beat inflation?

You want your portfolio's dividends growing faster than CPI, so aim for at least 6% a year when inflation runs around 3%. That gap gives you real income growth even after a bad year or a dividend freeze.

Is SCHD or VIG better for beating inflation?

VIG usually raises its dividend faster but starts with a yield under 2%. SCHD pays more now and raises more slowly. If you have 15 or more years, VIG's growth tends to win, and if you need income sooner, SCHD makes more sense. Plenty of investors hold both.

Should I hold dividend stocks in a taxable account or an IRA?

An IRA or Roth IRA is usually better, because dividends in a taxable account are taxed every year even when you reinvest them. If you run out of tax-advantaged space, qualified dividends still get the lower capital gains rates in a taxable account.
